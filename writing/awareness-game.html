Designing a security awareness game: how Crack the Code works

How a rebus puzzle game and a quiz can make awareness training stick, and why every message should end with an action staff can take.

For our company's Cyber Month, I designed a security awareness game called Crack the Code, a rebus puzzle game, and we ran it alongside a Kahoot quiz. Building it taught me a lot about what makes awareness training work. This post explains how a game like it is put together.

Why a game instead of a slide deck

Most staff have sat through awareness training that tells them not to click links. They nod, and they forget. A game changes two things. People have to work something out for themselves, and they remember what they worked out. It also turns security into something colleagues talk about together rather than something done to them.

How a rebus puzzle works

A rebus represents a word or phrase with pictures, symbols and letters. For example, a picture of a fish next to a fishing hook and an envelope could stand for phishing email. To solve it, players have to think about the term, and the answer becomes the start of a short conversation about what it means.

When I chose the terms, I followed three rules:

  • Every answer must lead to an action. Solving "phishing" should end with what to do about it, not just a definition.
  • Keep puzzles fair. A puzzle that is too clever makes people feel shut out. A few easy ones early on keep everyone playing.
  • Mix the topics: phishing, passwords, social engineering, safe browsing and reporting incidents.

Fit the message to what staff can actually do

Generic awareness material often tells people to install updates or antivirus software. In our environment, staff cannot install or uninstall software. IT handles that. Telling people to do something they are not allowed to do confuses them and weakens every other message.

So each answer in the game ends with an action staff can take, and the main one is the same every time: if something looks suspicious, report it to the InfoSec team. One clear action, repeated, is easier to remember than ten pieces of advice.

Reinforce it with a quiz

The Kahoot quiz worked well alongside the rebus game. The puzzles introduced the ideas, and the quiz checked whether they had stuck, with real scenarios: a suspicious email, an unexpected request for a password, an unknown remote access request. A quiz with a leaderboard also adds friendly competition, which keeps people engaged until the end.

Tips if you want to run your own

  • Base the scenarios on threats your organisation actually sees.
  • Check every piece of advice against your own policies before you use it.
  • Keep sessions short. A focused twenty minutes beats an hour.
  • Make reporting the hero of every answer.
  • Ask for feedback afterwards, and use it next time.

Summary

  • Games make people work out the lesson themselves, which helps them remember it.
  • A rebus puzzle turns security terms into a shared challenge.
  • Every message should end with an action staff are actually allowed to take.
  • A quiz afterwards checks what stuck.
